Submitted proof to audit
A398769 | Arbitrary fixed seven-adic depth
Proof proposed for independent repository audit, 25 November 2026.
Let $L$ be the number of decimal digits and $i$ the position of the nonterminal five, counting the units digit as position zero. The other five is at position zero and every other digit is seven. Write
$$
S=7L-4,\qquad F(L,i)=7\cdot10^L-18\cdot10^i-25,\qquad n=F(L,i)/9.
$$
Theorem. For every fixed integer $k\ge1$ and every nonempty open interval $I\subset(0,1)$, infinitely many distinct odd lengths $L$ admit an integer $3\le i<L$ for which
$$
i/L\in I,\qquad 225S\mid F(L,i),\qquad v_7(F(L,i))=k.
$$
Here $v_7(F)$ is the largest exponent $e$ for which $7^e\mid F$. Thus the full digit sum divides the decimal number and the seven-adic depth can be prescribed exactly. Taking $I=(41/100,42/100)$ answers the fixed-depth question left open in pull request 12. Taking other intervals also works, including intervals where an earlier theorem rules out full witnesses. The original witness condition requires $225S7^{L-2}\mid F$. The construction first fixes $k$ and then allows $L$ to grow arbitrarily large.
1. Two independently varying exponents
Fix $k\ge1$ throughout. Write $T_j=6\cdot7^{j-1}$ for $j\ge1$. This is the order of 10 modulo $7^j$, since 10 has order six modulo 7 and $7\mid10^6-1$ exactly once. In particular, 10 generates all units modulo $7^j$.
Set $H=7\operatorname{lcm}(16,T_{k+1})$. Its factor of 3 is exactly 3. Choose positive integers $A,B$ with $3^A\equiv1\pmod{H/3}$ and $17^B\equiv1\pmod H$. They exist by coprimality. For independent nonnegative integers $m,j$, set
$$
a=12+Am,\qquad b=1+Bj,\qquad S=3^a17^b,\qquad L=(S+4)/7.
$$
The seed has $S_0=9{,}034{,}497$ and $L_0=1{,}290{,}643$. On the whole grid, $S\equiv S_0\pmod H$. Indeed the residues agree modulo $H/3$, and both vanish modulo 3. Hence $L$ is an odd integer and $L\equiv L_0\pmod{\operatorname{lcm}(16,T_{k+1})}$. Also $L\equiv16\pmod{27}$, by $7L=4+S$.
2. The three congruences on the position
Power of 3. Put $u=((7\cdot10^L-25)/9),2^{-1}\pmod{3^a}$. The division by 9 is in the integers. Then $3^{a+2}\mid F(L,i)$ exactly when $10^i\equiv u\pmod{3^a}$. The order of 10 modulo $3^a$ is $3^{a-2}$ for $a\ge2$, from $v_3(10^h-1)=2+v_3(h)$ for positive $h$. Its powers are all residues congruent to 1 modulo 9. In fact $u\equiv1\pmod{27}$. To check this, the order of 10 modulo 243 is 27, $10^{16}\equiv145\pmod{243}$, and $7\cdot145-43=4\cdot243$. Consequently there is one exponent class modulo $3^{a-2}$, and it is 0 modulo 3.
Power of 17. Since $L\equiv3\pmod{16}$, we have $10^L\equiv14\pmod{17}$. Also $10^7\equiv5\pmod{17}$ and $7\cdot14-18\cdot5-25=-17$. Thus the target $(7\cdot10^L-25)/18$, with division modulo $17^b$, reduces to $10^7\pmod{17}$. The order of 10 modulo 17 is 16, since $10^8\equiv-1\pmod{17}$. Also $10^{16}\equiv137=1+8\cdot17\pmod{17^2}$. The binomial expansion lifts the order to $16\cdot17^{b-1}$, the full number of units modulo $17^b$. There is one exponent class giving $17^b\mid F(L,i)$. It is 7 modulo 16, in particular odd.
Exact power of 7. For the fixed length residue of this grid, there is a unique exponent class modulo $T_k$ giving $7^k\mid F$. All its members are 3 modulo 6, because $10^i\equiv-1\pmod7$. Of its seven lifts modulo $T_{k+1}$, precisely one gives divisibility by $7^{k+1}$. Fix any one of the other six, writing it $e\pmod{T_{k+1}}$. The frozen length congruence makes the same $e$ give valuation exactly $k$ at every grid point.
The first and second exponent periods are coprime. The third shares 3 with the first and 2 with the second. The residues agree at those overlaps by the preceding calculations. The Chinese remainder theorem gives a single class of exponents satisfying our three chosen conditions. Denote its least nonnegative representative by $r_{mj}$. Its period is
$$
W_{mj}=\operatorname{lcm}(3^{a-2},16\cdot17^{b-1},T_{k+1})
=16\cdot7^k\cdot3^{a-2}17^{b-1}.
$$
Here the period specifies our chosen one of the six exact-depth lifts. The other five are unnecessary.
3. Compatibility and a closed set on the circle
Increasing either grid index preserves the older residue class. To see this, use primes to denote parameters at the higher grid point. The integer $L’-L=(S’-S)/7$ is divisible by $3^a$ and $17^b$, and also by 16 and $T_{k+1}$ from the fixed length congruence. Therefore $10^{L’}\equiv10^L$ modulo $3^{a+2}$ and modulo $17^b$, by their multiplicative orders. The chosen exponent class at 7 stays fixed as well. Thus any exponent satisfying the higher conditions satisfies the older ones. Uniqueness gives $r’\equiv r\pmod W$.
Let $U=3^A$, $V=17^B$, and let $x_{mj}=r_{mj}/W_{mj}$ as a point of the circle $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$. The periods multiply by $U$ and $V$ respectively on increasing their indices. Consequently
$$
U x_{m+1,j}=x_{mj},\qquad V x_{m,j+1}=x_{mj}\quad(\bmod 1).
$$
Let $E$ be the intersection, over $N\ge0$, of the closures of the sets of points $x_{mj}$ with $m,j\ge N$. This is a nonempty compact set. Both circle maps, multiplication by $U$ and by $V$, take $E$ onto itself. For example, a sequence with both indices tending to infinity can be shifted down one index to prove forward invariance. Shifting it up and taking a convergent subsequence provides a preimage in $E$.
4. The accumulation set is infinite
Suppose $E$ were finite. Multiplication by $U$ would permute its points. Each would satisfy $(U^h-1)x=0\pmod1$ for some $h>0$, so a positive integer $D$ clears every denominator in $E$.
Fix $0<\varepsilon<1/(U+1)$, also smaller than $1/2$. For some $N$, every $D x_{mj}$ with $m,j\ge N$ lies within $\varepsilon$ of zero modulo 1. Otherwise a subsequence violating the condition with both indices tending to infinity would have a limit in $E$. Fix $j=N$ and let $z_m\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$ be the signed representative of $D x_{mN}$. Since $U z_{m+1}-z_m$ is an integer of absolute value less than one, $z_m=U z_{m+1}$ exactly. Thus $W_{mN} z_m=C$ is constant on this row. It is an integer since $Wz_m\equiv Dr\pmod W$. In particular
$$
D r_{mN}\equiv C\pmod{3^{a-2}}
$$
for arbitrarily large $a$, with $D>0$ and the same possibly negative integer $C$.
Here is an elementary contradiction. Write $X=10^L$ at each point of that row and $C_+=\max(C,0)$, $C_-=\max(-C,0)$. The order of 10 gives $10^{Dr_{mN}}\equiv10^C\pmod{3^a}$, with negative powers interpreted by inversion. The full power-of-three condition gives $7X-25\equiv18\cdot10^{r_{mN}}\pmod{3^a}$. Also $X^7=10^{4+S}\equiv10^4\pmod{3^a}$. It follows that the fixed integer polynomials
$$
P(X)=X^7-10^4,\qquad Q(X)=10^{C_-}(7X-25)^D-18^D10^{C_+}
$$
have a common root modulo $3^a$ for arbitrarily large $a$. They must have a common complex root. Indeed, if they were coprime over the rationals, the polynomial Bézout identity, after clearing denominators, would express a fixed nonzero integer as an integer-polynomial combination of $P$ and $Q$. Arbitrarily large powers of 3 would divide that integer.
The polynomial $P$ is irreducible over the rationals. For its positive root $t=10^{4/7}$, the number $t^2/10=10^{1/7}$ has degree 7 by Eisenstein’s criterion at 2 applied to $X^7-10$. Thus $t$ also has degree 7. If $P$ and $Q$ shared a root, $P$ would divide $Q$. At every root $x$ of $P$ one would then have $(7x-25)^D=18^D10^C$. But for a primitive seventh root of unity $\zeta$,
$$
|7t\zeta-25|^2-|7t-25|^2=350t(1-\operatorname{Re}\zeta)>0 .
$$
Their $D$-th powers cannot have the same absolute value. This proves that $E$ is infinite.
5. Recovering an actual position
Furstenberg’s topological theorem says that an infinite closed subset of the circle invariant under multiplication by two multiplicatively independent integers greater than one is the entire circle. Its hypotheses hold for $E$, $U=3^A$, and $V=17^B$. Hence every open arc contains points $x_{mj}$ with both indices arbitrarily large. The closed-set statement in Bourgain, Lindenstrauss, Michel and Venkatesh, section 1.1 · Furstenberg’s original paper, part IV
The ratio of the period to the length tends to a finite positive constant at this fixed depth:
$$
\frac{W_{mj}}{L}=\frac{112\cdot7^k}{153}\frac{S}{S+4}\longrightarrow\lambda_k=\frac{112\cdot7^k}{153}.
$$
Choose $t\in I$ so that $t/\lambda_k$ is not an integer and set $h=\lfloor t/\lambda_k\rfloor$. Use tail density to take a sequence $x_{mj}\to t/\lambda_k-h\in(0,1)$, with both indices tending to infinity. The actual integers $i=r_{mj}+hW_{mj}$ satisfy
$$
i/L=(h+x_{mj})W_{mj}/L\longrightarrow t.
$$
Hence $i/L\in I$ and $3\le i<L$ eventually, at infinitely many distinct lengths. The three congruences give $9S\mid F$ and $v_7(F)=k$. Both power-of-ten terms in $F$ are divisible by 25, and 25 is coprime to $9S$, so $225S\mid F$. This proves the theorem.
